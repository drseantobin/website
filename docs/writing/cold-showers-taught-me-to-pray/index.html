Cold Showers Taught Me to Pray

Learning to breathe, until I found I was being breathed

There is a version of the spiritual life that is really just self-improvement wearing better clothes, and breathwork is where I finally caught myself doing it.

I got interested in the science of breathing a couple of years ago. The Wim Hof Method, the vagus nerve, the way conscious breath can quiet the whole stress response. And then, being who I am, I sat down and wrote about it. A long essay, dense with citations. Aquinas on the unity of body and soul, the Catechism on contemplative prayer, all of it marshaled to prove that this practice could be made to serve God. I built a cathedral of argument around my own lungs.

Then I saved the file and never published it. It sat in a folder for over a year.

It took me an embarrassingly long time to see why. The essay was an attempt to master the subject. And breath is the one thing in you that was never yours to master in the first place.

The last frontier of self-improvement

We have optimized almost everything. Sleep has a score now. Focus has an app, and the app has a subscription. So it was only a matter of time before we came for the breath.

Breath is the perfect final conquest, because it sits on the one border in the body that runs both ways. Your heartbeat you cannot touch. Your digestion you cannot touch. But breathing is the single involuntary function you can seize with your conscious mind and drive. This is why it works, and why it tempts. Here, at last, is a piece of the automatic self you can take the wheel of.

In the clinic I watch the shadow side of this every week. The anxious person is, almost always, someone who cannot exhale. They breathe high and shallow into the top of the chest, bracing, as if the next inhale might not come unless they personally arranged it. What I am looking at is a nervous system that has decided it must hold its own life together by force. And the way out is never more thinking. You cannot reason someone out of anxiety, because anxiety does not live in the argument. It lives in the body. The work is to get them out of the head and into the body, which is the one place they have been refusing to go.

The first breath was not yours

Scripture does not treat breath as a technique. It treats it as the most intimate thing God ever did.

In the beginning the ruach of God, the breath-wind-Spirit, hovers over the dark water (Gen 1:2). Then God kneels in the mud. “The LORD God formed man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and man became a living being” (Gen 2:7). Read it slowly. This is not a word spoken across the void. This is God face to face with a fistful of clay, close enough to share breath, close as a kiss. You did not earn your first breath. Someone bent down over the dust of you and gave it.

Every breath since has been the same, only we stopped noticing. The Hebrew ruach and the Greek pneuma both mean breath and Spirit at once, and this is not a coincidence the ancients were confused about. They were telling us where to look. The desert fathers tied the Jesus Prayer to the breath, “Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God” on the way in, “have mercy on me, a sinner” on the way out, until it ran on its own beneath everything. St. John Climacus wrote, “Let the remembrance of Jesus be present with your every breath.”

And here is the part I kept trying to argue past. When Jesus dies, John does not write that he stopped breathing. He writes, “he bowed his head and gave up his spirit” (Jn 19:30). Handed over his breath. The last free act of the incarnate God is not to seize control of his lungs but to give them back. Then, on the far side of the resurrection, he does the first thing again. “He breathed on them and said, Receive the Holy Spirit” (Jn 20:22). The Giver breathing life into the dust a second time.

You cannot achieve your way into that. You can only receive it, or refuse it.

Out of the head, into the body

So I stopped writing about it and started doing it. Cold showers first, then a breathwork class with my friend Shereen Yusuff, a certified breath coach whose own book carries a blessing from Wim Hof in its foreword. And the thing my dense essay could not do, my body did in a few weeks.

The cold was the teacher. Standing under water cold enough to make everything in you want to flee, the only way through is the few things you actually can do: slow the breath, bring the heart rate down, and tell the body, beneath words, that it is safe, that it is allowed to be still. I was not conquering the cold. I was resetting a nervous system that had spent years braced. That is the shift I spend my clinical days trying to give anxious people, the one they can never quite reach from the outside. The cold reached it from the inside.

And it dropped me out of the noise of the skull and into what the tradition calls the heart, the deep center where a person actually stands before God. It turned out the Lord was already there. This is where it parts ways with the Eastern thing, the dissolving of the self into some impersonal calm. It was the opposite. Not absence but presence. Not emptying but relationship. The practice did not erase me, it made me present enough to be met.

That is when I understood what my cathedral of argument had missed. These techniques are sacramental. They work on the body and the nervous system, and for that very reason they make the spirit present. It is praying with the body, on purpose, and it has a form the way the rosary has a form. The repetition is not the point. The repetition is the trellis the presence climbs. Shereen’s book, Seeking the Spirit Within: Experiencing Union with God Through Breath, does this on the page. A convert and a Benedictine oblate, she takes the discipline she has spent years teaching and sets it inside a Catholic understanding of the person, tying the breath not to some technique of self-mastery but to the heart and the indwelling Spirit.

The Catechism draws the line I kept smudging. Contemplation, it says, is the prayer of “the forgiven sinner who agrees to welcome the love by which he is loved” (CCC 2712). Agrees to welcome. Not attains. Thomas Merton said it with a bluntness I needed and resented: “True contemplation is not a psychological trick but a theological grace. It can come to us only as a gift, and not as a result of our own clever use of spiritual techniques.” I had written a manual for the clever use of spiritual techniques and called it reverence. What I actually needed was to get in the cold water and be still.

Giving it back

So breathe. Do the cold, slow the exhale, learn what the vagus nerve does. None of it is the enemy. But be clear about what you are actually doing, because the wellness industry and the anxious heart will lie to you in the same direction. They will tell you that you are gaining control. You are not.

You are practicing the return.

Every conscious exhale, if you let it be, is a small rehearsal of the last one, when you will hand the whole borrowed thing back to the One who breathed it into you in the dark. “The Spirit helps us in our weakness, for we do not know how to pray as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with sighs too deep for words” (Rom 8:26). That verse used to frustrate me. Now I think it is the most freeing sentence in the New Testament. Prayer was never mine to perform. It is happening in me already, under the words, in the one rhythm I did nothing to start and will not decide to end.

You were never breathing on your own.

You were being breathed.

If this gave words to something you have been carrying, share it with someone who is holding their breath.
