The Monks Are Not Doing Anything Special

A 30-year study found men in monasteries outlive other men by five years. The reason is not what you would sell.

A demographer in Vienna has spent nearly thirty years counting the dead in monasteries.

Marc Luy began the German-Austrian Cloister Study at the Vienna Institute of Demography in 1997, and it now holds the life records of roughly 16,600 monks and nuns across Germany and Austria, with a few in Switzerland and Italy, some of those records reaching back to 1890. The finding making its way around this week is that men in religious orders live about five years longer than men outside them. It is being handed to us as a health story, which is the one thing it is not.

The number underneath the number is the one that matters. Out here, women outlive men by roughly six years. Inside the cloister, that gap nearly closes to one. Luy’s conclusion is that biology accounts for about a single year of the difference between men and women, and that the rest of it, the whole remaining stretch, is not written into the male body at all. It is written into the way men live.

Which means the monastery is not adding five years to a man’s life. It is declining to take them.

There is an obvious objection here and it deserves its due. Nobody randomly assigns men to monasteries, and the man who enters one at twenty and stays for life is unusual before he ever arrives, spared the hazardous work and the hard drinking that take other men early. That is the right challenge to bring, and it is the reason Luy set monks beside nuns rather than beside the general population. Same roof, same rule, same food, same hours, one variable left. Inside that comparison the male disadvantage very nearly disappears.

Somebody notices early

The researchers name plain things. A fixed daily order, the same hours for work and meals and prayer, kept whether or not anyone feels like keeping them. Stress that does not accumulate in the body the way it accumulates elsewhere. Prayer, which turns out to be legible to demographers as emotional stability. Relationships measured in decades, because leaving is not a live option. Work aimed at something the worker did not invent for himself.

Then there is one more, and I have not been able to put it down. In a monastery, when a man begins to fail, somebody notices early. That is in the findings. Decline gets caught sooner because the community is watching. Nobody is being suspicious of him. These men have eaten breakfast with him every morning for forty years, and they can tell.

Outside those walls, a man can come apart slowly and nobody will mention it. Not because no one cares for him. Because no one is close enough, often enough, on a fixed enough schedule, to see the drift while it is still small.

I have watched this in my own work for years. The men who arrive in my office are almost never in an acute crisis on the day they call. They are years into something. They have been sleeping badly since a date they can name. They have been drinking a little more since a job ended. The weight came on, the friendships thinned out, the checkups got postponed, and at no point in that long descent did a single person say, I have noticed something in you and I am not going to let it pass. Nobody abandoned them. Nobody ever stood close enough to abandon them, and a man can lose a decade in the gap between those two things.

We have built substitutes, and they are very good. A ring that scores his sleep. An app that counts his steps and congratulates him. A watch that will tell him his heart rate is elevated and mean nothing at all by it. A man can now be measured continuously and known by no one.

Nobody enters a monastery to live longer

Not one of those men took vows in order to add five years. The longevity is residue. It collected at the bottom of a life aimed at something other than itself, and it collected there because nobody was reaching for it.

That should stop us, because the response to a finding like this is immediate and predictable. Strip the monastery for parts. Take the 4am rise, the cold room, the silence, the fasting, the routine, and bolt them onto an ordinary ambitious life as a performance upgrade. We already have a name for it. Monk mode: a stretch of maximum output with the distractions cleared out.

A monastery is not a productivity protocol with incense. Benedict opened his Rule by calling it a school for the Lord’s service (Rule of St. Benedict, Prologue), and every element the optimizers want to extract is there in service of a surrender they have no intention of making. The monk did not build that schedule. He submitted to one built by someone else, and he cannot leave it when it stops serving him. He does not own his time, his work, his income, his location, or his future. He is under obedience to a man he did not choose, in a community he did not select, doing work assigned rather than pursued, for a purpose that will never carry his name. He is, by every measure our culture uses, radically unfree.

And he outlives us by five years.

What we are actually protecting

I do not think men are dying early because they lack information. Every man I know can recite what he should be doing. He is dying early because the life that would keep him alive asks him to hand over the one thing he has organized his whole existence around keeping.

It requires being under something. Hours he did not set and cannot renegotiate on a bad morning. Work whose value does not accrue to him. People standing close enough, and licensed enough, to say out loud what they can see happening to him. And staying put long enough that leaving stops being the reflex whenever things get uncomfortable. Every item on that list is a subtraction from autonomy, and autonomy is the thing we have taught men to defend above their own bodies.

I recognize the pattern from the inside. I work largely alone, and my hours are mine, which is another way of saying nobody is owed them. There is no hour of my day that another person is expecting me to keep. Nobody is coming to my door at six to see whether I am at prayer, and if I quietly stopped, the news would reach no one. I have defended that freedom for years. The cost of it runs slowly, and I am the only person positioned to notice it, which is the exact arrangement the monastery refuses to allow.

“Whoever would save his life will lose it,” Jesus said, “and whoever loses his life for my sake will find it” (Mt 16:25). We have spent a long time treating that as an exclusively spiritual accounting, a ledger settled somewhere beyond death. The demographers have now measured a small corner of it in years, and the arithmetic runs in the same direction. The men who handed their lives over are the ones still walking the corridor at ninety, and whatever else is going on in that number, it is not nothing.

The five years do not live in the routine. They sit on the far side of the surrender that makes the routine possible, and there is no version of that you can buy, install, or run for six focused weeks. What is on offer in there was never a better morning. It is a life that has stopped being an asset under management, and a room full of men who would notice, tomorrow, if you did not come down.

If this gave words to something you have been carrying, share it with someone who needs it.
