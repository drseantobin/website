The First Generation That Cannot Tell a Child What He Will Be

The fear about machines and jobs is an identity crisis arriving at the scale of a civilization, and the bill comes due before the unemployment numbers do.

A great many people are afraid that a machine is about to take their job, and nobody can tell them how much of that fear is warranted, including the people building the machines. What we do know is the pattern, because it has held for every technology we have ever built. The loom put the weavers out, the tractor emptied the fields, the spreadsheet retired a generation of clerks, and every time the crowd that said no to the machine was right about the losses and wrong about what came after.

So when someone says no to automation, the question worth asking is who benefits from the no, because keeping a thing slow and expensive rarely serves the person doing the work and almost never serves the poor, who are the first to gain whenever anything gets cheap. Technological advance has been the engine of ordinary wealth for as long as anyone has kept records, and you can win that argument in about four minutes, which is why so many smart people keep having it. It is the smaller argument, though, and the larger one is about who we are becoming while the work changes hands. Either we spend what the machines hand back on family and friendship and the people down the street, the things that make a human life good, or we spend it guarding what we already own against a future we would rather not let arrive.

What a man is actually mourning

What interests me is what people ask for when the fear surfaces. They ask for wages and protections and guarantees, because those are the things that fit on a sign. Let a man talk long enough and something else comes up: the men he works beside, his father doing the same job, the son he assumed would follow him into it, the fact that everyone in one particular building knows who he is the moment he walks in. The negotiation is always conducted in money. The grief underneath it almost never is.

We are all descendants of people whose trades vanished, and we do not spend a single evening mourning them. We spend those evenings asking when something will get cheaper, which is another way of asking when a human being will finally be removed from making it. All of that is true and almost entirely beside the point, because nobody has ever been talked out of grief with a spreadsheet. When a man loses his work, the thing that takes him apart is rarely the money. It is that he no longer has an answer to the only question anyone asks at a party.

How far back the name goes

Smith. Miller. Baker. Fisher. Taylor. Cooper. Wright. For most of the history of the West a father handed his son a name and a trade in the same motion, and the name was the trade, and nobody experienced this as a limitation. We are the first generation that cannot tell a child with any honesty what he will be doing at forty. That is not a labor statistic. It is an identity crisis arriving at the scale of a civilization, and the emotional bill for it will come due long before the unemployment numbers do.

The three things we decided would stay expensive

There is a second thing shaping the mood of everyone under thirty. Nearly everything we have permitted technology to make cheaper has become absurdly cheap: the phone in your pocket that could not have been built at any price when I was a boy, food from the other side of the world in February. The three things still crushing young people are housing, health care, and education, and not one of the three is expensive because physics demands it. A young man can cross a major city for fifteen dollars in a car with nobody driving it, and an ambulance ride five blocks can take his savings. That gap is a failure of nerve among the people steering, and if we hand the next generation miraculous conveniences and unreachable staples, we should not act surprised by what they do about it.

By nearly any measure you can name, this is the best moment in human history to be born, and I can prove it on paper and still fail to make you feel it. We have built a device that pipes the worst thing that happened anywhere on earth into a child’s hands for nine hours a day, and then we stand in his doorway asking why he seems so sad. We have lost the ability to imagine a good tomorrow, a strange condition for a people whose history with technology is so genuinely glorious. Nobody hates antibiotics, or refrigeration, or the plane that brings a daughter home for Christmas. What we hate is the screen, and what it has done to our attention, and what it is doing to our kids.

The question being handed back

Hold the identity crisis and the good news in the same hand, because something is hiding in the overlap. If work is losing its power to tell us who we are, we are also being handed back a question almost nobody in history has been permitted to ask. We are about to have the time, the wealth, and the freedom to actually choose, and I do not think we have taken seriously how rare and how dangerous that is.

Dangerous because freed time does not spend itself well by default. It goes to the screen unless something better is waiting, and nothing better is waiting unless we build it on purpose. Rare because the things that were always the point are within reach of ordinary people: friendship that goes past the surface, prayer with enough silence around it to be worth something, children raised by parents who are genuinely present rather than merely in the house, the corporal works of mercy done with your own hands for a person whose name you know, and work taken up because it is good rather than because it pays, which is what the word leisure used to mean before we turned it into recreation.

None of that is a consolation prize for the job you lost. It is the thing the job was always supposed to be buying you, and most of us never got close enough to notice. If the machines take the toil, the question they hand back is the oldest one there is, and we have been too busy and too tired to answer it.

Busy and tired are the polite reasons, and the less polite one shows up when you watch where the energy goes once the ground starts to move. It goes into wealth and power and the guarantee that neither will be touched, which is why the price of a house never comes down in a country that keeps its savings in the house, and why the no to automation and the no to cheaper housing turn out to be the same reflex wearing different clothes. The man in Christ’s parable who pulls down his barns to build bigger ones is laying up security for years he does not have, and the question he hears that same night is whose it will all be (Luke 12:20). I know the reflex from the inside, having measured more than one year by what it added to an account rather than by whether my children got more of me. A country does the same thing when it spends a windfall on insurance against change, when the whole point of the wealth was the family and community it was meant to make room for. Adapting costs less than guarding, and it is the only one of the two that leaves a person free to become someone.

So the crisis is real, and I would not soften it. A man who does not know what he is for is in serious trouble, and we are about to have a great many of them. The answer will not arrive as a better job title. It will come from the places that have always carried it and that we have been treating as decorative for a generation now: a table with people at it, a parish, the friend who notices you have gone quiet, ground under your feet and weather on your face. God did not redeem us with a concept. He did it with a body, in a particular town, at a specific hour, among people who knew his trade and his father’s name.

We can solve the future of work and still lose. Solve the future of living and I suspect the rest of it follows.
